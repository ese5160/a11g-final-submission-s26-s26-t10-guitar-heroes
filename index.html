FretNought

A smart guitar that shows you how to play —
LEDs under the strings, an IMU in the pick, and a backend that listens for the strum.

01

The Project

FretNought is a smart guitar learning system that places addressable LED strips beneath the strings and lights up the exact frets a beginner needs to press for any chord. A wireless smart pick — worn like a wristband — detects strumming through an onboard IMU, while a microphone on the fret device listens for the guitar itself.

The two streams meet in a Node-RED backend, which only advances to the next chord when motion and sound arrive within a 500 ms window. Beginners stop translating chord charts in their head and just play.

02

Two Modes

Practice

Learn it slow.

Pick a chord. The fretboard lights up. Strum once — confirmed by the pick's IMU and the mic together — and the next chord lights up. No metronome, no pressure.

Performance

Play the song.

Set tempo, capo, and a chord progression. FretNought walks the fretboard through the song in time, so you can play full pieces without ever looking at a chart.

03

System Architecture

Two SiWx917 modules — one in the fretboard, one in the pick — talk to a Node-RED backend hosted on Azure. The backend fuses sensor events, drives the dashboard, and streams chord state back to the fretboard.

Fret Device
  • SiWx917 Wi-Fi MCU
  • Addressable LED strips (SPI)
  • I²S microphone for audio detect
  • OTA firmware updates
Smart Pick
  • SiWx917 Wi-Fi MCU
  • IMU over I²C (QWIIC)
  • Wrist-worn enclosure
  • Strum-event publisher
Backend
  • Node-RED on Azure VM
  • MQTT message bus
  • Sensor fusion (500 ms window)
  • Web dashboard UI
04

Challenges

a.

Timeout 102

The custom SiWx917 PCB refused to flash over SWD, throwing a persistent Timeout 102 error. We exhausted the obvious fixes — jumper rework, POC reconfiguration, GPIO pull-up audits, probe swaps — and ultimately migrated firmware to dev boards to ship the project on schedule.

b.

Three drivers, three buses

With the dev boards in hand, three peripherals needed bring-up. The I²S mic came up by configuring receive mode at the right word size and DMA'ing samples for frequency analysis. The I²C IMU needed a register-level driver for control setup and polled accelerometer reads. The SPI LED strip was driven by hand-crafting MOSI bit patterns at a clock rate that encoded the LED protocol's exact pulse timing.

c.

The LEDs that wouldn't go dark

Even with the LED driver working, the strip never fully turned off — a faint glow persisted. Tracing the issue revealed our update code was running in a thread without the privileges to fully control the SPI peripheral's idle state. Granting the right privileges drove the line clean and let us turn the strip truly black.

d.

Steel strings, exposed pads

Mounted under the strings, the LED strip's exposed pads kept shorting against the steel strings during play. Tape and a heat-sink layer fixed it for the demo — a permanent fix would mean a properly insulated LED housing in the next revision.
